What we actually make

Rytmi is a playout engine, in software. Syke is its clock.

There are two conventional places to take playout timing from, and both leave the card's own clock unmeasured. Pace from the host and you assume the SDI card agrees with it; lock to house sync and you assume the reference will always be there. Either way nobody is watching the crystal in the card, and it is a few parts per million off — ours by about −3.7 ppm through 1 Aug 2026, on the card the first unit then carried, which is one slipped frame every hour and a half or so if nothing corrects for it.

Syke paces from TAI — a monotonic timescale with no leap seconds — and measures the card's drift continuously rather than assuming it away. Rytmi's timing authority is Syke's, and Syke's is TAI. Every schedule item airs against an absolute instant, not an offset from whenever the process happened to start — with seven known exceptions, each in the register below:

  • A looping region airs its first item twice per loop. Fixed on the first unit since 3 Oct 2026, where its test passed, read 6 Nov 2026; the second unit has no fix.
  • A schedule whose first item lay more than a minute ahead of the engine’s start aired that item at once, early. Since 5 Oct 2026 the first unit holds such an item, and since 5 Nov 2026 it no longer airs it up to a second late; neither unit’s schedules start that far ahead, so neither fix has been seen on air. The second unit has neither.
  • On the first unit every item aired 41–58 ms after its instant. Fixed there on 9 Oct 2026, with two further faults the fix brought out, and its test passed: in the run of 5–6 Nov 2026 every one of the 372 starts read was on its instant. The second unit has none of these fixes and was not measured.
  • A fixed-time item that follows a source faster than its channel airs late — 2.5 and 3.9 s in two tests without a card. On the first unit since 6 Nov 2026 a faster source keeps its scheduled length, and the same two tests then aired the fixed-time item on time; that has not been measured on air. The second unit has no fix.
  • On the first unit, from 3 Oct to 6 Nov 2026, an item due less than a frame after the one before it ends could start at once, up to a frame early, and over several items that end a little short this could put a frame or two of emergency material between programmes. Since 6 Nov 2026 such an item airs on its own frame there, the frame before it held once. The second unit’s build never had that rule.
  • A step in the system clock after the engine starts moves what airs. The first unit’s build, since 11 Oct 2026, carries the step to the next fixed-time item; the second unit’s airs fixed items late for minutes after a forward step and freezes after a backward one. Since 5 Nov 2026 the first unit’s build no longer freezes after a backward step longer than it has been running, nor airs an item again after one; tested without a card, as no step has happened on air.
  • After a restart the second unit plays its schedule again from the first item, whatever the time, and every item after it is late until the next fixed-time item. Since 6 Nov 2026 the first unit’s engine cuts back in where the schedule is; that unit’s own schedules have not exercised it on air.

Which is why, by design, losing house sync would not cost you your time base. Take the second of those approaches and the reference is your timing authority, so when it goes you are left on an uncorrected crystal. Syke's authority is TAI, and Rytmi's is Syke's, so the card's rate error is a measured quantity rather than an assumption, and we predict its consequences from that figure: to within 1 % at the median over the twelve-hour runs of July 2026 (individual runs −8 %/+19 %), and to 14 % over the 339-hour run of August, where the sheds arrived sooner than the model said because the drift number we publish is a run-cumulative mean that lags the crystal (median observed ÷ predicted 0.863 over 213 intervals, re-derived 21 Aug 2026).

Genlock is something the card can take and we do not depend on: the design rule from the start was that the clock must not rely on it, and until 27 Sep 2026 the first unit had never been genlocked. Through the 339-hour soak that ended on 20 August 2026 the card reported no late and, after start-up, no dropped frames of its own; the engine's own late-frame count is carried in the panel above while a soak is running, and explained in the table below when it is not zero. We first put the design to the test on 27 Sep 2026, as far as our own instruments reach; the register below has what that showed, what locking cost here, and what it did not cover.

The as-run log is a hash chain. Each record commits to the one before it, blocks are sealed and fsynced, and rotated day files are verifiable across boundaries. It is written as the engine plays, not assembled afterwards, so changing an earlier record breaks the chain for anyone who already holds a hash from later in it. Since 3 Oct 2026 the first unit’s log also records each airing of a schedule item, and signs each seal with a key made on that machine. As of 6 Oct 2026 nobody outside Karhu TV can check those signatures, the log carries no anchor outside itself, and our backups of it are mirrors that a later copy overwrites: the hashes are plain SHA-256, so someone holding the day files could rewrite a record and recompute every hash after it, in that file and every later one, and only a hash kept out of their reach would show the change. What verifying the log proves, and what it does not.

And it is software, not an appliance. The machines behind the on-air figures on this page are two built around stock Intel desktop processors, each with a DeckLink card any broadcast dealer stocks; the figures we mark as measured without a card come from our development laptops, stock parts too. Their motherboards, memory and drives are standard retail parts too, listed part by part with the software versions each machine runs. The nanoseconds come from measurement, not from custom silicon — the engine treats every clock in the box as something to verify rather than trust, which is why it holds time on hardware you can buy, replace, and keep spares for. When a card fails, you swap a card — not a channel appliance.

One price, everything enabled, bought once. There is no feature matrix, no per-channel tier, no per-output unlock and no annual renewal: the licence is perpetual and the software you install is the whole software. We price this way because the alternative — charging separately for the things a playout system already has to do — makes the vendor's incentives point away from the operator's. It also means the number we quote is the number, which is not universally true in this market.

Four independent SDI outputs per card, first driven from one process on 5 Sep 2026 on the second unit, which has since run all four unattended: 221 hours unbroken, 18–27 Sep 2026, and 433 hours, 27 Sep–15 Oct 2026, until the crash in the register below — every output playing the same mixed-source schedule at one output rate, the engine counting no late frame, though its alarm for a decoder falling behind stood on two of the four outputs for most of those 433 hours.

Two limits, each in the register below: a 220 ms stall on 14 Sep 2026 cost three outputs their card buffer, and stalls on 30 Oct 2026 on the second unit and on 2 Nov 2026 on the first did the same, dropping frames at the output until the buffer refilled or we restarted the engine (open); and a source faster than its channel’s output rate airs slow on the second unit, while on the first unit, since 6 Nov 2026, it airs at its own speed with fewer of its frames.

Runs on Ubuntu. Binary-only, under NDA — the timing core is the company, so it isn’t published.

After the run: what fourteen days of logs were worth

The campaign missed its own criterion on 11 August, and a failed test deserves more time than a passing one would have got. When the run ended we re-read the fortnight from eight independent directions — the clock's own record, the compliance log, the host's counters and the archive — and most of the value is in what that ruled out.

The two late frames turned out not to be two events. They are the largest members of a population the instruments had been recording all along and nobody had counted, because the counter that trips at half a millisecond only ever saw the two that crossed it. They are all in one direction, they are all close to one size, the clock is back inside its ordinary range within the following second every time, and every one of them is independently visible in the as-run log — which is worth knowing about a compliance log, because it was not built to be a timing instrument.

What that buys: it is a repeatable mechanism rather than noise; it is bounded — under 3 % of one frame at its worst over the 339-hour run of August 2026, 4.5 % on the 228-hour run that ended 4 Sep 2026 — and none of it reached the SDI output; and it is not caused by the things it would be convenient to blame. Around twenty candidate causes have now been tested and rejected, several of them ours — and on 21 August the last two that were still standing went the same way. As of that day what was left was not a shorter list of the same kind of suspect, and the next step was a measurement rather than a patch; the changes we have made since, and why we credit none of them, are in the “Worst single tick” row above.

The same logs carry both halves of this run: the junctions that were exact and the wakes that were not. We still have not found the cause, and this section will not carry a story in place of one. What changed is that the question got small enough to answer, on instruments that were already running.

What we don't claim, why, and what would earn it

In April we decided internally not to ship HEVC. The website went on advertising it for three more months. Nobody caught it, because nothing was watching — and a claim nobody is watching is indistinguishable from a lie to the customer who relies on it.

This is what we keep instead of that habit. Every row is a thing we could plausibly say and don't. It is on the public site rather than in an internal file, because a register that lives where customers can't read it is the same failure with better manners.

Licensing

HEVC / H.265 in Rytmi

Out of scope in Rytmi on patent-licensing grounds since April 2026: not claimed, tested or marketed for playout. Karhu Player decodes HEVC on the computer’s graphics hardware only, never in software (its page).

Would earn it: licences from the HEVC patent pools and a hardware test pass. Neither is planned.

Measured — but only as far as our own instruments reach

Glass-to-glass latency

We measured the engine-side component over 200 trials on 30 Jul 2026. It is a lower bound: it stops at the point the frame enters the handoff queue, and the card pipeline beyond that point adds at least 60 ms this number does not include — the card buffer measured 8 frames, 160 ms, unchanged across 1,824 samples over 30 h to 1 Aug 2026. Scheduled-event accuracy on the wire is likewise unmeasured; engine-side, the first frame of a scheduled item left our converter 22–36 ms after its due instant at the junctions measured on 30 Jul 2026, on code since corrected (RET-146, near the top of this page). On 9 Oct 2026, after those fixes, the first unit’s own log put all 20 items of a schedule’s first pass on their instants, which is still engine-side. Quoting it as glass-to-glass would understate our own latency by an unknown margin.

Would earn it: a capture rig on the SDI output measuring the round trip end to end.

“Never drops a frame”

The first unit’s card at the time had a crystal that ran about 3.7 ppm slow against TAI over 26 Jul–1 Aug 2026, so the engine produces marginally faster than the card consumes. A surplus frame is discarded from the handoff queue roughly every 90 minutes at the drift measured through 1 Aug 2026 — by design, and at a rate we predicted from the measured drift to within 1 % at the median over July 2026's twelve-hour runs (individual runs −8 %/+19 %) — and 14 % over the 339-hour August run, median observed ÷ predicted 0.863 across 213 shed intervals, re-derived 21 Aug 2026. The card itself reported zero late and zero dropped frames after start-up, across the whole 339-hour run. Locked to a house reference, discards follow the reference's drift rather than the crystal's, and on our bench on 27 Sep 2026 they came more often; the holdover row below has the figures. Whether a discarded frame is visible on the wire is engine-side accounting, not a wire measurement. Separately, frames have been dropped at the card when a stall cost an output its buffer; the stall row below has those.

Would earn it: the same capture rig. Until then we report what our counters see and say which side of the card they sit on.

Holdover through a reference failure

First locked on 27 Sep 2026, and the reference pulled the same day. Until then the card's genlock state had read unlocked in every one of 78,132 samples on the first unit since it began publishing on 2 Aug 2026. Set to 1080i50, a Blackmagic Mini Converter Sync Generator was detected by the cards in both our units, the first and a second, and in 4.5 minutes of watching neither locked to it. Set to 720p50, both locked to it from 13:59 UTC — five running outputs between them, every one at 1080p50. Whether an output running 1080i50 locks to a 1080i50 reference is untested. Two cards whose crystals free-run more than 4 ppm apart then agreed to within 0.038 ppm (medians over 3.57 h). Locking to this generator had a cost, and we publish it. Syke keeps to TAI; a locked card keeps to its reference; and this generator ran about 11.8 ppm slow against TAI, further out than either card's own crystal. That is measured the same way as the crystal figure in the evidence table and carries the same caveats; Blackmagic publishes no accuracy figure for the generator to set it against (checked 16 Sep 2026). The difference arrives as surplus frames, so the engine discarded one about every 29 minutes while locked (median interval, both units) against 28.2 predicted from that drift — nearly four times as often as the first unit shed them free-running, where the median interval was 110 minutes over 9–14 Sep 2026. That cadence belongs to this generator; another reference would give another. While locked, neither the engine nor the card counted a late frame. Then we pulled it. At about 17:44 UTC we cut the generator's power with every channel on both units running. Both units read unlocked at the next once-a-minute sample; the engine counted no late frames, and its worst clock tick of the run did not change; each card was back at its own crystal's rate within the first full minute; the second unit's intervals between discards went back to a median of 47 minutes across its four outputs that evening, against 46 predicted for its crystal; and the loss paged the operator's phone from both units — the first time that alarm had had anything to fire on. All of that is our own instruments. What the SDI output did across either edge is not measured. Acquiring the reference stepped every output's hardware clock, as the card reports it, by more than a frame against TAI, while the engine's clock was undisturbed and our late, discard and dropped-frame counters did not move — so none of our instruments can tell you whether a viewer would have seen it. Losing the reference showed no such step. And it was one loss, once: enough to say what happened that afternoon, not to promise it every time. The genlock-source setting in our configuration is still read by nothing, as of 27 Sep 2026: the cards locked without any instruction from our software.

Would earn it: part of this is earned — lock observed, then the reference pulled on running channels and the edge watched by our own instruments, 27 Sep 2026. What remains is the wire: the same capture rig on the SDI output while a reference is gained and lost — and more than one loss.

Audio continuity across clip changes

For sixty days, from a change we made on 18 Jul 2026, the engine fed the card audio at the wrong rate after any clip change where the source frame rate changed but the audio channel count and sample rate did not. Fed too fast, the card refused the surplus; fed too slow, it ran short. Recorded on the wire on 16 Sep 2026, the worst case put 5.2 % silence, in short scattered gaps, into programme audio that had none. The defect was in the software that ran the 339-hour run above; what it did to that run’s audio was not measured, and that run’s frame and junction figures are video figures. Fixed on both units on 16 Sep 2026. Over two runs of the fixed build on the second unit totalling 256 hours, 16–27 Sep 2026, the card refused none of the engine’s audio on any of its four channels, by the engine’s own count.

Would earn it: the output’s audio recorded across every junction of a full pass and compared with the source, rather than a counter that stayed at zero. The counters that found this one lit up within three minutes of first being switched on.

Sound in step with its picture

Three faults of ours have put a clip’s sound out of step with its picture, and none has been measured on the wire. At the decoder’s output, read without a card on 4 Oct 2026, a clip’s sound ran one frame behind its picture (20 ms at 50p, 40 ms at 25p) and its first frame was silent; the first unit has carried a fix since 9 Oct 2026. A frame the engine discards to absorb the card’s drift, on 25p or 29.97 material on a 50p channel, moved the sound against the picture by 20 ms either way, at random; the first unit has carried a fix since 5 Oct 2026, and read on 10 Oct 2026 the 40 ms steps that marked it in the card’s own count of the sound it holds were gone from its discards. That count cannot settle the rest: it also swings by about 100 ms from hour to hour, before the fix and after, which may be how the card counts its sound or may be the sound itself. And a clip begun part-way through a group of pictures — a playlist joined in progress, a trimmed start, the cut-in after a restart — aired its sound two frames behind its picture, read without a card on 9 Nov 2026; the first unit has carried a fix since that day, and none of its schedules had such a start on air when we looked. The second unit has none of these fixes. Every figure here is the engine’s or the card’s own.

Would earn it: the output’s sound measured against its picture on the SDI output, at a clip’s start, across a discard and at a start part-way through a group of pictures.

An engine that does not crash

At 08:58 UTC on 7 Oct 2026 the first unit’s engine crashed, and both of its channels were off air for at least 13 seconds until the service manager restarted it. Nothing paged us; we found it a day and a half later, reviewing our own instruments. It crashed inside Intel’s video driver, which does not keep the fetch of a decoded frame from the graphics memory apart from the teardown of the decoder that made it — and our engine let the two overlap at the end of a clip. A probe of ours, outside the engine and run without a card on a development laptop, crashed the same way in ten runs out of ten, and not once in 35,000 tries with the two kept apart. The same day the same fetch failed once without a crash, and one interlaced frame aired built from one source picture instead of two. Since 16:18 UTC on 9 Oct 2026 the first unit’s engine keeps the two apart, and since that day a crash on the first unit pages us, with the kernel’s own line for the fault. In the week from then to 16:18 UTC on 16 Oct it aired through seven deliberate restarts with neither a crash nor a failed fetch — the week we said we would accept the fix on. Against the two events in the 64 hours before it, a quiet week comes by chance about one time in 200: evidence that the fix holds, not proof that the engine never stops. On 12 Oct 2026 we found one way past that guard, reproduced without a card: a stream whose coding parameters change part-way makes FFmpeg tear the decoder down inside a call the guard did not cover. The first unit’s engine covers it since 5 Nov 2026. The second unit has no such fix, and on 15 Oct 2026 at 15:43 UTC, after 18 days on air, its engine crashed at the same place in Intel’s driver, on a progressive channel this time, a sixth of a second before a programme change. With no core dump kept, that it was the same overlap is inferred from the thread it crashed in. All four of its channels were off air for at least 16 seconds until the service manager restarted it, and it paged us only that its alarms had cleared; since 18:27 UTC that day a crash on the second unit pages us too. Separately, the first unit’s engine crashed four times at a planned stop, after its output had ended, inside the SDI card’s library (1 Aug–3 Nov 2026); its as-run log came through each. Since 6 Nov 2026 that engine waits for its card threads before it exits, and each of its four planned stops from then to 10 Nov 2026 was clean. The second unit has no such change.

Would earn it: the same fixes on the second unit, and the 90 days of uptime the row below asks for.

Loudness monitoring on air

Since 3 Oct 2026 the first unit measures each airing’s integrated loudness and true peak, to BS.1770 and EBU R128, on the audio it hands on towards the card — after the output mix, not from the source file — and writes both into the as-run log’s record of that airing. Against FFmpeg’s own measurement of the same stretch of the same file, without a card, on 3 Oct 2026, every airing we could compare agreed within the tolerances of EBU Tech 3341. It is measured inside the engine, not at the connector; since 6 Nov 2026 the first unit’s engineering page lists each airing’s loudness and true peak once the airing has ended, but nothing shows it while the item airs, and the operator’s page does not show it; and the second unit has none of it. Until then this row said we had “a full BS.1770 loudness meter in the codebase”. It was not full: its “true peak” was a sample peak, and it gave the 5.1 surround channels the wrong weight. Both were fixed on 3 Oct 2026, and no figure we published came from it.

Would earn it: the figure on an operator’s screen while the item airs, and the same figure read by a meter on the SDI output.

Subtitles carried through to the output

Since 10 Nov 2026 the first unit’s second channel carries the OP-47 subtitles a file holds into its SDI output’s ancillary data, one packet a frame, beside the timecode, and its as-run log records each airing that carried them. The only file in that channel’s schedule that holds subtitles is a test pattern of our own, a teletext page of nine subtitles over ten seconds, which airs each time the engine starts. Structural, as the system stands today

Item-level granularity in the as-run log

The as-run is a tamper-evident hash chain and we stand behind it: 120,483,920 records checked as of 21 August 2026, no false positives, six forgery vectors closed, known breaks from before the 339-hour run of August 2026 — one between two day files (20→21 June 2026), and others inside day files, among them 20 and 21 June and 17 July 2026. It records one entry per channel frame — one per source frame where a source runs at its channel’s rate, and fewer entries than the source has frames where the first unit airs a faster source (the as-run page has the detail) — and that entry is not exact about what played or when: a fault in our log, found in July 2026 and open as of 6 Oct 2026. Each entry is written as the engine hands its frame on towards the card, and stamped with the time of the engine clock tick the frame was made for rather than when the frame leaves the SDI output (the as-run page has the detail). In the log as it was written until 3 Oct 2026, and as the second unit still writes it, there is no item boundary — so two consecutive plays of the same file read as one continuous run of double the length — and a frame the engine then discards to absorb the card’s drift keeps any entry it had, so the log can say a frame played that never reached the card. That is not hypothetical: RET-122 airs the head of a looping region twice per loop, as it did on the first unit through the 339-hour run of August 2026, and that log cannot show it. Since 3 Oct 2026 the first unit’s log records each airing of a schedule item, so a second play is a second airing, and withdraws the entry of a discarded frame whose picture does not air; that every discard is accounted for is not established. The chain proves its records are the ones written, in the order written; it does not prove they were true, or that nobody rewrote them all — and we would rather tell you which is which than let the first imply the rest.

Would earn it: an item-boundary record — on the first unit since 3 Oct 2026, through the versioned format change this row asked for. Still to earn: frame entries written from what the card reports it played rather than from the hand-off, and a run of 50p material only, under a test written down beforehand, in which every discard is withdrawn.

Every item airs on its instant

The design is that every schedule item airs against an absolute TAI instant, not an offset from whenever the engine started. We know of seven exceptions, in the order found:

  • A looping region airs its first item twice per loop, and displaces what follows (RET-122; the row above has what the log could show of it). The first unit has carried a fix since 3 Oct 2026, and its test passed there, read 6 Nov 2026: the loop goes round with no second play of its head, by the engine’s own count. The second unit has none.
  • A schedule whose first item lay more than a minute ahead of the engine’s start aired that item at once, early by almost the whole lead (RET-142, found off air on 4 Oct 2026). Neither unit’s schedules start that far ahead. Under the first unit’s fix, since 5 Oct 2026, such an item waits, but until 5 Nov 2026 it aired late by however much of a second the engine’s start left before its next whole second: 110 to 757 ms in eight tests without a card on 11 Oct 2026, up to a second in all. The fix for that, made on 11 Oct 2026, has been on the first unit since 5 Nov 2026; in three tests without a card it aired such an item on its time, and no schedule there starts far enough ahead to show it on air. The second unit has neither fix.
  • On the first unit every item aired 41–58 ms after its instant (RET-146, found on 8 Oct 2026; the median of each of its eleven runs of 5–9 Oct 2026, some items a further 40 ms later, the figure set partly by when each run started). Frames were stamped a frame late, and handed to the output a moment after the tick that should have aired them. The first unit has carried a fix since 00:33 UTC on 9 Oct 2026. On the fix, 77 of the 80 items of a schedule’s first pass in its first four runs started on their instants to within a millisecond, and three — the same item each time — 20 ms early: a second fault, in which a looping region never took a fresh time from the schedule, so the part-frames left over where a 29.97 clip was cut added up until an airing ended 20 ms short. That was fixed on the first unit at 20:13 UTC the same day, and in that run’s first pass all 20 started on their instants. The fix also cut two frames from each of the first eleven items after a start on a schedule with no times; that was fixed on the first unit at 11:20 UTC. Its test passed there, read 6 Nov 2026: in the run of 5–6 Nov all 372 starts read, 18 of them fixed-time items, were on their instants. The second unit runs the code from before all of this, with none of these fixes, and was not measured.
  • A fixed-time item that follows a source faster than its channel airs late: it waits for that source, which plays slow, and is late by as much as the source has fallen behind — 2.5 and 3.9 s in two tests (RET-128, found without a card on 13 Oct 2026). On the second unit that holds for every faster source. On the first unit since 6 Nov 2026 a faster source airs for its scheduled length, and the same two tests, again without a card, then aired the fixed-time item on time; a source whose stated frame rate disagrees with its average still airs slow there, and a fixed-time item after a faster source has not been measured on air.
  • An item due less than a frame after the one before it ends started at once, on the first unit from 3 Oct to 6 Nov 2026: a gap shorter than a frame was not a gap, so an item between half a frame and a frame past its programme change started at the change, up to a frame early. After several items each ending a little short, that could add up to a frame or two of emergency material between programmes (the archive entry of 15 Oct 2026 has how we found it). Since 6 Nov 2026 such an item airs on its own frame there, the frame before it held once. The second unit’s build never had that rule.
  • A step in the system clock after the engine has started, measured without a card on 10 Oct 2026. On the build the second unit runs, every time the engine publishes stays on the offset it took at its start: after a 2 s forward step it aired fixed-time items late for about three minutes, by up to the step, and after a backward one its output froze for the step’s length. The first unit’s build, since 11 Oct 2026, takes a step on its next frame and records it in the log; the items after it air early or late by the step until the next fixed-time item, which airs on its time. Until 5 Nov 2026 a backward step longer than the engine had been running still froze that build’s output, and an item could air again after one; since 5 Nov 2026 the first unit’s build does neither, tested without a card, as no step has happened on air.
  • After a restart the second unit plays its schedule again from the first item, whatever the time — a crash, a power cut or a deliberate stop alike — and every item after it is late until the next fixed-time item. Since 6 Nov 2026 the first unit’s engine cuts back in where the schedule is, and since 9 Nov 2026 it joins an item begun just before the restart and records the items it passed over, or says why it cannot (the archive entry of 9 Nov 2026 has how it works, and the fault its test missed). That unit’s own schedules have not exercised it on air.

Would earn it: every one of these fixes on both units, each accepted on air.

A faster source plays at its own speed

A source faster than the channel’s output rate airs slow, by the ratio of the two rates (0.834× for a 59.94 source on a 50 channel, 4 Aug 2026, RET-128). On the first unit since 6 Nov 2026 it does not: each channel frame — each field, on an interlaced channel — shows the source frame due at its time, so the source airs at its own speed and for its scheduled length, with fewer of its frames and less even motion. In the 24 hours from that evening a 29.97 clip on the first unit’s 1080i50 channel aired 264 times, each for exactly its 750 frames, where the build before had aired it for 899. A source whose stated frame rate disagrees with its average still airs slow. The engine does not refuse a faster source; on the first unit it reports each one in its log, at start-up and when a playlist is loaded while it plays. The second unit has none of this: it airs every faster source slow, and flags one in its log only for the items it checks at start-up — by default the first 30, and any due within 30 minutes.

Would earn it: the same on the second unit; and a fixed-time item after a faster source measured on time on air.

Four outputs ride out a stall

On 14 Sep 2026 a 220 ms stall during a test of ours cost three of the second unit’s four outputs their card buffer, and an ordinary clip change twelve minutes later began an hour of frames dropped at the card; we restarted the engine before the buffer came back. It has happened twice since, with no test running. At 13:53 UTC on 30 Oct 2026 the second unit’s card took no frame from any of its four outputs for about 200 ms: 9,550 frames were dropped at the outputs, the last at 15:15 UTC, about three minutes of pictures across the four, and each output ran a frame late for 27 to 74 minutes; by 19:35 UTC every buffer had come back without a restart, as the card’s clock drift refilled it. On 2 Nov 2026 the first unit’s first channel lost its buffer the same way in a 300 ms stall, and dropped about 1,700 frames, most of them in a five-minute burst after we changed a memory setting to stop it recurring, before we restarted its engine. Both stalls began while monitoring tools of our own were loading large log files into a machine short of free memory, and that is the cause we infer; it is not established. We have since made those tools read less, and set both units to keep more memory free.

Would earn it: the cause found, and a stall repeated on purpose that the outputs ride out without dropping frames.

Cross-family rates on one card while genlocked

Mixed source rates into one output rate are supported and normal, and the picture is proven on hardware — the sound across a change of source rate carried a defect for sixty days, found and fixed on 16 Sep 2026 (the audio-continuity row above). Until 5 Oct 2026 the engine bound one output rate per box; from then the first unit’s engine runs rates that divide one another from one tick, and the second unit’s still binds one. As of 27 Sep 2026 no 1001-family (NTSC) frame had been on the wire from it. This row is about one specific edge, so here is the whole map rather than a caveat that sounds bigger than it is.

The genlocked cross-family cell is an inference, not a measurement: one reference input feeds the whole card, and whether two of its outputs can lock in different families has not been tried. A house reference has now been on this bench — on 27 Sep 2026 every running output on both units locked to one, as the holdover row above sets out — but all of them at one rate, 1080p50, since the engine then bound one output rate per box; two rates from one tick under a reference have not been tried. Until 8 Oct 2026 this row said the same-family cells were not built and that the engine binds one output rate per box; the first unit had run them since 5 Oct. The free-running cell is our limit, not the card's: below the master clock the pipeline is already per-channel, and two Duo 2 cards have each accepted 50 and 59.94 on two outputs at once (14 and 17 Sep 2026), one of them completing frames at both rates together, 50.25 and 60.24 a second by its own count (18 Sep 2026) — the card’s own callbacks, not a receiver on the wire. What is single is our master tick, and one tick has to divide exactly into every rate it serves. 59.94 is not 60 — it is 60000⁄1001, and 1001 shares no factor with 60000, so nothing cancels. The smallest tick that lands on both is 60 kHz (120 kHz once 23.976, which we also admit, is on the grid — ruled 18 Aug 2026): 1,200 ticks per 50 Hz frame, 1,001 per 59.94 — twelve hundred times today's grid resolution, but only 2.2× its wake rate, because you visit a grid point only where a frame boundary falls: 109.9 wakes a second against 50 today, derived 7 Aug 2026 and re-derived 16 Sep 2026. (Were the second rate an exact 60, it would be 300 Hz and this row would not exist; the whole cost is the NTSC 1001.) That is an engineering choice of ours, not a law of physics — and on 16 Sep 2026 we ruled mixing 50 with 59.94 out of our next clock, with the conditions for re-opening it written down. The obstacle is spacing: at 50 and 59.94 two frame boundaries can fall 16.7 µs apart, far closer than our clock’s own timing margin, and copying one frame’s worth of picture in memory took about 243 µs when we measured it that day. A separate cadence source per channel is the route we know, and it is deferred; rates that share a denominator — 25 with 50, 29.97 with 59.94 — are what that clock is scoped to mix. We would rather say that than let a decision of ours read as a hardware limit.

Would earn it: genlocked cross-family — a second card with its own reference, and a clock that can run two rates in one box. Free-running cross-family — a clock per channel, which we have deferred; a finer shared tick is not the route, for the reason above. Ask if you need it; the answer is a scope, not a no.

Not earned

Uptime and reliability figures

Our internal threshold for saying “reliable” is 90 days continuous. The longest run we can evidence as of 29 October 2026 is 433 hours — just over eighteen days, on the second unit, and it ended in a crash (the register’s crash row); the service manager and the engine’s frame counter agree on it, and its as-run log has not been verified over it. The longest the as-run chain itself evidences is 339 hours, on the first unit in August. These figures are dated on purpose: runs get longer, and a number stated as current would be wrong the next time one does. This row said 39 hours until 21 August, which was true when written on 1 August and had been beaten twice before anyone re-derived it, and 339 hours until 29 October, after the 433 had stood for two weeks. What does not move is the argument — 433 hours is not 90 days, and until it is we do not use the word.

Would earn it: 90 days. There is no shortcut and we won't paraphrase our way to one.

ATSC A/85 loudness conformance

A/85 assesses the anchor element — dialogue — not integrated programme loudness, which is what our meter measures. For dialogue-sparse content the two differ materially. The 2026 revision also asks for true peak to be measured before encoding, which a delivered file no longer permits. The loudness code in Rytmi therefore returns INCONCLUSIVE for A/85, and cannot return a pass: the refusal is enforced in code, before any number is compared. No tool of ours that runs it is released.

Would earn it: dialogue-gated measurement. A different algorithm, not a threshold change.

If a claim you need isn't here and isn't on the evidence table, ask — the answer will be a measurement, a date, or “we don't know”.
